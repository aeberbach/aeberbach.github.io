Reworking brake hall effect modules

October 28, 2026 4 min read

(The model that this article is about can be downloaded at my Printables)

If you're building the rudder pedals you might like to see a little more detail on the hall effect modules that make the brakes work. The brakes operate by pushing the top of the pedal so that the pedal rotates around its spindle, just like in a car. Inside the rotating part is a magnet that comes closer to the hall effect sensor causing the output voltage to change.

The hall effect sensor mounts with one M3 bolt inside the pedal collar. This collar fits over the steel pedal shaft and attaches to the arm using two M3 bolts. By loosening these bolts the collar can be rotated, and since the collar includes blocks that define the limit of travel for the pedal, that changes the angle of the pedal at rest.

In the full video showing construction of the rudder pedals I used some plain strip board to mount the hall effect sensor, a 3-legged device in a case very similar to the familiar TO-92 transistor - though it is actually called a "LPG" package. The problem with strip board is that the bolt coming down through the strips can create electrical circuits where they should not exist. To try and avoid that I beveled the hole drilled for the bolt and made sure to put the strips on the opposite side to the bolt head.

On updating my second rudder unit (it's a long story, but two different printers, workshops, countries even) I found a better way. It starts with a different pattern of prototype board.

There is no continuous circuit here, just a bunch of solder positions. If the bolt head comes down on them it may bridge adjacent pads but will not create a short on the other end of the board. This is really convenient for what I'm doing here because it allows me to solder the sensor in place, add a capacitor, wire the right connections to the pads at the edge of the board and thereafter just solder to those pads.

I cut a little rectangle from the edge of the board with a dremel cutoff wheel, keeping three of those big useful solder pads and 3x5 holes. In the central row, second hole from the pads, I drilled a larger hole for the M3 bolt. With the bolt in place this positions the last three holes so that a hall effect sensor soldered here is positioned correctly to pass through the slot and be in the right position to sense the magnet in the pedal assembly. After a little bit of cleaning with a file it is ready to assemble.

The datasheet for the DRV5056 contains the note "TI recommends connecting this pin to a ceramic capacitor to ground with a value of at least 0.1µF." - so, I added a 0.1uF SMD capacitor, size 0805, between these pins. (There's enough room for a through-hole part, if you use a small one.) If I was then trying to solder to the pins of the sensor directly this would be a problem because the capacitor can easily get hot enough to melt solder on both sides, then you have a capacitor stuck to your soldering iron and have to start again. Mounting the hall effect sensor, soldering 30 gauge kynar-insulated wire to the pads and then adding the capacitor between 5V and GND was a one-time job. Then the wires get soldered to the pads and some kapton tape ensures that they don't spring up immediately the solder on the pad is melted.

After that it's easy - with the module installed in its pedal collar, the wires that come down through the pedal arm can be tacked to the solder pads. After folding the wire so that there is enough slack to allow rotation of the collar for pedal angle adjustment it is loosely fastened to the arm, and the pedal assembly can be fitted with the spring and completed.
